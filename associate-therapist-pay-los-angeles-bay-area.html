The question, as associates keep asking it

The private practice offers 20 to 25 clients a week at a higher per-session rate, pays admin hours at $18–20, and doesn’t add up to a 40-hour week. The nonprofit offers $70,000–$75,000 for a 40-hour week regardless of caseload, with 10 to 25 clients a week. Why would anyone take the private practice offer?

The reframe

It is not one question. It is four, and only one of them is about salary.

Compared year by year the two offers are close enough that the choice looks like temperament — security against upside. Compared across the road to licensure they are not close at all, and the thing that separates them is not the money. Here is what actually decides it.

1Is the offer lawful on its face?

Two of the numbers in that post are already under a California floor. $70,000 is $304 below the 2026 salaried-exempt threshold, and $18 an hour is below the City of Los Angeles minimum wage from 1 July 2026. Neither is a dealbreaker; both are questions you are entitled to ask before you sign.

2How many direct clinical hours, really?

Not clients seen — hours the Board will credit as direct clinical counseling. Above about 17 a week, the statutory 104-week minimum takes over and working harder does not license you sooner. Below it, every hour is a week. “10 to 25 clients” is the widest possible answer to the most important question.

3What does the non-session time pay?

An associate cannot be a 1099 contractor in California, so both offers are W-2 and the self-employment-tax argument does not apply. What differs is whether notes, no-shows, meetings and the supervision the Board requires are paid at all. Per-session pay is piece rate; the law says those hours are paid separately.

4Which one is a qualifying employer?

A 501(c)(3) or county employer opens PSLF, HCAI’s LMHSPEP, and BH-CONNECT loan repayment of up to $180,000 that names associates by registration type. A private practice opens none of them. For an associate carrying graduate debt this is worth more than the whole salary difference, several times over.

Check one

Three floors an offer has to clear.

None of these is advice about which job to take. They are the checks that turn “does this feel fair?” into a question with an answer.

The salaried-exempt floor: $70,304

California requires an exempt salaried employee to be paid at least two times the state minimum wage for full-time work. In 2026 that is $16.90 × 2 × 2,080 hours = $70,304 a year, or $5,858.67 a month. It is the same figure everywhere in California, because it tracks the state minimum wage and not the local one.

Per-session pay is piece rate, and piece rate has rules

First, the rule that surprises people: a California associate may not be an independent contractor. Not a preference — the Business and Professions Code says it outright for all three registrations, and requires you to hand the Board a W-2 for every year of experience you claim. So the usual 1099-versus-W-2 arithmetic, where the contractor eats both halves of FICA, is not part of this comparison at all.

The same sections bar an associate from taking payment directly from clients, from holding any proprietary interest in the employer’s business, and from renting space or paying for furnishings, equipment or supplies. If an offer asks you to cover any of that, it is not a hard bargain; it is outside the statute.

Second: a flat sum per completed session is piece-rate compensation. Under Labor Code §226.2 the employer must pay separately, at no less than the applicable minimum wage, for all other non-productive time — progress notes, treatment planning, no-shows and late cancelations, staff meetings, training, intake calls, and the weekly supervision the Board requires. Rest breaks are paid separately again. The session rate may not be averaged across those hours to reach the minimum.

The arithmetic that follows

A practice paying $35 a session and nothing for the twenty minutes of notes after it is very likely underpaying, and Labor Code §1194 makes the unpaid balance recoverable with interest and attorney’s fees — notwithstanding any agreement to work for less.

Which is why the good offers already look like this: piece rate for sessions, a separate hourly rate for everything else. An offer that pays $18–20 for admin is not being generous. It is being lawful — and in the City of Los Angeles, $18 no longer is.

Check two

What these jobs actually pay, by name.

California requires employers with fifteen or more staff to publish a pay scale in a job posting, so most of this is on the record rather than crowdsourced. Public agencies publish salary schedules that will still resolve in a year; the nonprofit figures are postings read in August 2026 and should be treated as dated observations.

Public agencies, and the finding nobody expects

County and city behavioral health pays associates more than nonprofits do, and in San Francisco it is not close. SF’s class 2930 starts at $118,820 for a post whose own specification accepts an ASW, AMFT or APCC registration — roughly $38,000 above what a San Francisco nonprofit pays the same registrant. Check three

Seventeen hours a week is where the argument ends.

An AMFT or APCC needs 3,000 supervised hours, of which at least 1,750 must be direct clinical counseling, gained over not less than 104 weeks, at no more than 40 credited hours in any seven days. Two years is the floor no matter what you do.

1,750 divided by 104 weeks is 16.83 direct clinical hours a week. Which produces the one piece of arithmetic that settles most of this decision:

The crossover

17 hours a week

At or above it, the calendar decides your licensure date and a heavier caseload buys you nothing. Below it, your caseload decides it, and every client you are not seeing costs you weeks. For an ASW the same sum on 2,000 clinical hours puts the crossover at 20 hours a week.

Which means the nonprofit’s “10 to 25 clients a week” is the single most important sentence in the offer, and it is the one part of it that has not been quantified.

Four things that quietly change the answer

Read these before you compare two caseloads

  • Clients seen is not hours credited. A no-show is not a direct clinical hour. Ask what the practice does when a client cancels — both for your pay and for your hours.
  • The 40-hour weekly cap is across all settings, not per setting. That is the Board’s reading in its own FAQs rather than the statute’s words, but it is the reading that governs in practice. Two part-time jobs do not get you there faster.
  • Past ten direct clinical hours in a week at one setting, you owe a second unit of supervision that week. A bigger caseload costs supervision time as well as clinical time, and supervision counts against the 1,250-hour non-clinical cap.
  • 500 of the AMFT’s 1,750 must be with couples, families and children. A setting that never sends you a family is a setting you cannot finish in. APCCs: this requirement was repealed effective 1 January 2022 and a good many websites still list it.

The site’s 3,000-hours calculator projects a real licensure date against all four gates from your own weekly numbers.

Check four

The number that is larger than the salary difference.

Two offers $5,000 apart is a real difference. It is not the same order of magnitude as this. California names AMFTs, ASWs and APCCs — by registration, not by license — in a loan repayment program worth up to $180,000, and it is only available if your employer is a Medi-Cal safety-net site. A private practice is not one.

Before you sign

Nine questions, and what a good answer sounds like.

Ask the private practice

  • What is paid for a no-show or a late cancelation? “Nothing” is an answer worth having in writing before you count on 25 sessions a week.
  • Which hours are paid at the non-session rate? Notes, treatment planning, staff meetings, training, intake calls and supervision should all be on that list.
  • Is supervision paid time? The Board requires at least one unit a week in every setting where you gain hours. It is not optional, so it is not your own time.
  • How many sessions has a full-time associate here actually held, on average, over the last three months? The number in the offer is a ceiling. This one is the floor.
  • Who pays for the EHR, the liability policy, and the phone? Labor Code §2802 says the employer reimburses necessary business expenses, and §4980.43.3(f) separately bars an associate from paying for the employer’s obligations.

Ask the nonprofit

  • How many direct clinical hours a week does someone in this post actually bill? Not the target — the realized number. This decides your licensure date more than anything else in the offer.
  • Is the post exempt or non-exempt? If it is under $70,304 it cannot be exempt in 2026, which means overtime past 40 hours. Get the answer in the offer letter.
  • Are you a 501(c)(3), and will you certify PSLF employment? The employer’s tax status is the whole test. Ask who signs the form.
  • Do you participate in BH-CONNECT or LMHSPEP, and is this site eligible? Site eligibility, not your registration, is usually the binding constraint.
