Examination statistics · Q3 FY 25/26 · checked August 2026

The pass rate is 84%. It is also 65%.

Both are the California LMFT Clinical exam. The Board publishes two rates for every exam and almost nobody quoting one of them says which. Here are seven quarters of both, for all seven exams.

The distinction everything turns on

Two rates. They are not close, and they answer different questions.

1The first-time rate — the one that answers your question

Only candidates sitting the exam for the first time. In Q3 FY 25/26 that was 742 of the 1,057 people who sat the LMFT Clinical exam, and 80% of them passed. This is the number that answers “if I sit it properly prepared, what happens?”

2The overall rate

Every sitting in the quarter, including second, third and later attempts. The same quarter’s figure was 65%. It is lower because retakes are harder than first sittings, not because the exam got harder. This number answers “what share of the exams sat this quarter were passed?” — a question about the Board’s workload, not about you.

3Which one you are usually shown

Whichever makes the point being made. The free analysis that circulates in study groups reports a figure in the high fifties and was last revised in 2017; the schools quote first-time rates. Neither is wrong and neither is the whole number. This page prints both every time, in that order.

The LCSW Law and Ethics exam has been reported at both ends of a twenty-three point range inside two years. Nothing about the exam changed by twenty-three points. A quarter is a small sample of a self-selecting group of people who chose that quarter to sit, and it should be read as one.

What the Board itself concluded

The Office of Professional Examination Services ran a five-year study of the low LPCC Law and Ethics pass rates. Its conclusion, printed in the February 2026 packet, was that “no statistically significant or specific factors were identified that could explain the observed lower pass rates”.

That is the most careful look anyone has taken at one of these numbers, and it found nothing to explain it. It is a good reason to be suspicious of any confident story about why a rate moved.

Established by absence

What the Board does not publish.

These are the questions people ask most often in study groups, and the honest answer to all of them is that the data does not exist publicly. Nine board packets were read for this page; none of them contains any of it.

The questionWhat is published
Which schools have the best pass rates?Nothing. The Board does not break any exam result out by degree program, school or accreditation status. Any ranking you find is somebody’s reconstruction, not a Board figure.
What is the pass rate on a second attempt? A third?Nothing. The split is first-time against all sittings. The retake rate can be inferred as a residual, but the Board does not state it and the inference is fragile at these sample sizes.
How many attempts do people usually need?Nothing. Not published in any packet read for this page.
What score do I need?Not a percentage. These are criterion-referenced exams scored against a standard, not curved against other candidates, which is why a practice-test percentage traded in a study group does not convert into a pass probability.
Does the pass rate differ by region, language or route to eligibility?Nothing, except that endorsed and out-of-state candidates are broken out separately in the FY 2025/26 packets only, and those cohorts are small enough that their rates swing wildly.

That last point is worth sitting with. The single most requested statistic about California licensing exams — which program prepares people best — has never been published by the body that has the data.

A documented failure, with numbers

Once, the passing score itself was wrong.

This is not a rumour from a support group. It is in the Board’s own May 2025 packet. A new publication of the LPCC Law & Ethics exam took effect on 1 February 2025 and was sent to the testing vendor with an incorrect passing score. The Board’s examination services unit caught it while reviewing results.

What the rescoring found

33 of 77

Of the 77 LPCC Law and Ethics exams taken between 1 February to 19 February 2025, 33 results were changed from fail to pass. The remaining 44 were unaffected. Candidates were notified by email, and exams sat from 20 February onward were scored correctly.

Nearly 43% of the people who sat that exam in that three-week window were told they had failed when they had passed.

The reason to print this is not that it is likely to happen to you. It is that the Board found and fixed it, published the numbers, and the only place those numbers appear is a meeting packet. If a result ever looks wrong, the quarterly examination report is where an answer would surface — and the packets are public.

The exams themselves are only half of the wait. Once you have passed, the Board still has to process the application, and that queue has moved a long way in the last year — the processing times are here. If you are still counting hours, the hours calculator works from your own numbers and sends nothing anywhere.
