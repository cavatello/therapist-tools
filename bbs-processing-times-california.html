Processing times · Q3 FY 25/26 · checked August 2026

You passed. Now how long?

In short

I passed the exam — how long until the license actually arrives?

The Board's published processing times, the year-over-year shift, and the cost of an application that comes back

96 days for an LMFT license

The Board publishes this every quarter and nobody reads it. In the last year getting registered got faster and getting licensed got much slower — an LMFT license went from 54 days to 96.

Registrations are moving in one direction and licenses in the other. The Board attributes the licensing side to staffing constraints and cross-training. Read these as the shape of the queue in a quarter, not as a promise about your file — a mean of several hundred applications says nothing about any one of them.

The thing worth knowing

Registration sped up. Licensure did the opposite.

1Getting registered is now quick

An ASW registration went from 29 days to 10 and an APCC registration from 46 to 25 over the year. If you are a new graduate waiting to start accruing hours, that wait is a fraction of what it was, and it is now short enough that the binding constraint is your transcript, not the Board.

2Getting licensed is not

An LMFT license went from 54 days to 96, and an LCSW license from 48 to 88. That is roughly three months of waiting after you have passed the exam, paid the fee and submitted a complete application. Plan the gap between the last supervised session and the first independent one around three months, not three weeks.

3LPCC and LEP are a different queue

An LPCC license was processed in 22 days and an LEP license in 11, both broadly stable. The volumes are far smaller, which is the likeliest explanation and is not one the Board has given.

The one variable you control

What a missing document costs you.

A “deficient” application is one the Board had to come back to you about — a missing transcript, an unsigned verification, a gap in the hours. It is not a rejection. It is a letter, a wait for your reply, and a return to the back of somebody’s queue.

The LEP figure of 186 days is an artifact the Board explains itself: one application that remained deficient until it expired at 365 days.

How often it happens

In Q1 FY 2025/26 the Board put the licensing deficiency rate at 29.74%, up from about 23% the quarter before. Roughly three applications in ten come back.

Which makes the deficient column above the more honest planning number for a lot of people, and makes the checklist below worth more than any amount of refreshing BreEZe.

The things that most often send an application back

  • Transcripts that have not arrived yet. The Board’s own explanation for a spike in deficient registrations was graduation-season timing — degrees conferred after the application went in.
  • An experience verification the supervisor has not signed, or has signed in a way the form does not accept. The Board does accept an electronic signature; it does not accept a typed name.
  • Hours that do not reconcile between the weekly logs and the verification total.
  • A registration that lapsed mid-associateship, leaving a gap the hours cannot cross.
  • Paper rather than BreEZe. The Board says online filing saves weeks against a paper application, and does not quantify it further — so treat that as a direction, not a number.

If you have seen different figures

The Board changed how it measures, and the two series do not join.

Through Q1 FY 2025/26 the Board published a single processing time per application type, covering every application it processed. From Q2 FY 2025/26 it publishes approved and deficient separately. Those measure different things, and the older numbers are still in circulation.

The same quarter, measured two ways

For Q2 FY 24/25, the packets published in 2025 reported an AMFT registration at 45 days on the old basis. The February 2026 packet, backfilling the same quarter on the new basis, reports 29 days.

Neither figure is wrong. One includes the applications that had something missing and one does not. Draw a single line through them and you get a fifteen-day improvement that never happened — which is why this page prints two tables and a paragraph instead of one chart.
