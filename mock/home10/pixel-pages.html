Everything after the degree is a second job nobody trained you for.

You spent two or three years learning to sit with people. In that time nobody explained what registration costs, how long 3,000 hours actually takes at your caseload, why California will not let you form an LLC, what an insurance panel pays against private pay, or what is left after tax once you finally have your own clients.

The answers exist. They are scattered across accountants who charge by the hour, forum threads written by people guessing, and blog posts that stop exactly where the arithmetic starts — and almost none of it is specific to California, which is where most of the difference is.

“What does the whole route actually cost?”

$575 if you finish in the statutory minimum, $875 if you take the full six years. Both are real. Which one you pay depends on how long you take, and nobody tells you that before you start.

“Which programmes publish their tuition?”

Thirty-three of the sixty-five. The other thirty-two want you to call and ask. Every one of the 65 is on this site either way, with its units and its format.

“Is this associate job worth taking?”

A 60/40 split at $120 a session is not obviously better than a $35 flat hourly rate. It depends on unpaid admin, whether supervision is counted, and how fast the placement generates relational hours.

“Why is my tax bill that big?”

On a $250,000 practice it is $69,410 — and $18,244 of that is optional, decided by an entity choice and a retirement account most people make once and never revisit.

The questions people actually email me

Is it really free?

Yes. All of it, with no account and no email required to see a number. Some links out to third-party services are affiliate links and are tagged where they appear; they cost you nothing and never change what a calculator here tells you.

Is this only for MFTs?

No. LMFTs, LCSWs, LPCCs, psychologists and registered associates. The licensure pages are MFT-specific because that is where the rules differ most — the money pages apply to any licensed California clinician in private practice.

Are you an accountant or a lawyer?

No, and none of this is advice. Every legal claim links to the statute so you can check it yourself, and every figure is arithmetic you could redo. Take it to a professional — you will just start much further along.

How current is it?

Every page carries the date it was last checked against its source, and there is a public changelog of every figure that has moved. The 2026 BBS schedule, the 2026 contribution limits and the 1.3% SDI rate are all in.

Why California only?

Because the parts that cost you money are the parts California does differently: no LLC for licensed services, an uncapped SDI charge on an S-corp salary, an $800 minimum franchise tax, and its own clinical exam. A national resource has to skip exactly those.

Do I have to sign up for anything?

No. There is an email list for when a number changes, and that is the only thing on the site you can sign up for.

California will not let you form an LLC for this.

Cal. Corp. Code §17701.04(e) blocks a limited liability company from providing services that require a licence. For an MFT the real choice is sole proprietorship or a professional corporation — and the corporation is a state filing, while the S-corp election is a separate federal tax choice on top of it. Anyone offering to set up an “LLC for your therapy practice” in California is describing something you cannot lawfully use.

How self-employment tax actually works, and why the S-corp saving is smaller than it looks

Self-employment tax is 15.3% on 92.35% of net earnings — 12.4% Social Security up to the wage base and 2.9% Medicare with no ceiling. An S-corp splits profit into salary (which pays it) and distribution (which does not), which is where the saving comes from. Two things eat into it: the salary has to be reasonable or the IRS can recharacterise the whole distribution, and California charges SDI at 1.3% on the entire salary with no cap — $1,248 on a $96,000 wage — which most comparisons leave out entirely.

  4. IRC §402(g) and Notice 2025-67 — the 2026 elective deferral limits.
