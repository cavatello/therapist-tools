How this actually works and what you give up
The mechanic, in one paragraph

As a sole proprietor you can open a Solo 401(k) — a retirement account for a business with no employees other than you and a spouse. You put money in before income tax is worked out, so your taxable profit falls by whatever you contribute. Federal tax, California tax and, on the employer portion, part of your self-employment exposure all fall with it. The money is not spent. It sits in an account with your name on it, invested however you choose.

Why the number is bigger than people expect

You wear two hats. As the employee you can defer up to $24,500 of your own money. As the employer you can add roughly 20% of net self-employment earnings on top. Added together the ceiling is $72,000 — far more than the $7,500 an IRA alone allows, which is the account most people have heard of and the reason they underestimate this.

The catch, stated plainly
  • It is deferred, not forgiven. You pay income tax when you take it out in retirement — the bet is that your rate then is lower than your rate now. At a high marginal rate that bet is usually good. Early in a career, on low profit, it often is not.
  • The money is locked up. Withdrawals before 59½ generally cost a 10% penalty on top of the tax. Do not contribute money you will need for a deposit, a slow quarter, or six months of expenses.
  • It does not reduce self-employment tax on your own deferral. Social Security and Medicare are worked out before this, so the saving is income tax, not all tax. Anyone promising otherwise is selling something.
  • Contributing lowers your reported earnings, which can slightly reduce the Social Security you are credited with later. Small, but real.
  • You have to open it before the year ends even if you fund it later, and the employer portion moves with profit — so a bad December changes the ceiling.
The alternatives, briefly

A SEP-IRA is simpler to open but has no employee deferral, so at the same profit the ceiling is usually lower. A SIMPLE IRA suits a practice with staff. A Roth version of any of these reverses the bet — no deduction now, no tax later. Most solo therapists with real profit land on the Solo 401(k); it is worth twenty minutes with a CPA before you do.

Most therapists know roughly how many clients they got, and almost nobody knows how many people looked. That gap is the whole game: if 200 people read your Psychology Today profile and two called, the profile is the problem, not your rate. If two people read it and both called, you do not have a conversion problem — you have a visibility problem. Those are opposite fixes, and you cannot tell them apart without the first number.

Employing associates — what you need to know

This is not a revenue split — it is payroll. In California a registered associate must be a W-2 employee (or a volunteer) and may not bill clients directly. Your practice bills the client, so all of it is your revenue, and the “split” is simply how you work out their wage.

What a wage costs on top of the wage
What the wage does not pay for

Progress notes, case management, client advocacy, treatment planning, no-shows and team meetings. For a non-exempt employee that time is still hours worked and still has to be paid — a share-of-collections wage that ignores it can fall below minimum wage once the real hours are counted, which is the practice’s problem, not the associate’s.

Sources

  • EDD, California payroll taxes — UI at the new-employer rate and ETT are employer-paid on the first $7,000 of each employee’s wages; SDI is withheld from the employee.
  • Workers’ compensation is mandatory for any California employee — the rate depends on your carrier and class code, which is why it is an input rather than an assumption.
