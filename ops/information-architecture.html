01

The pattern every door follows

This is the actual proposal. The five mockups further down are the same six blocks with different content, and if the pattern is right they will all work.

What shipped first, and was wrong

“One bar. Four gates. Nothing you type here leaves this browser.”

A headline describing a widget, two of four blocks about privacy, and four figures that were the statute — numbers the reader already knows. Written for somebody arriving from a link in a group, already oriented. A hub over twenty pages read as one calculator.

What it says now

“Everything a California associate needs, in one place.”

The offer in the headline, the audience in the line above it, the scope in the line below, and figures about the resource: 20 guides, 58 portals checked, 165,000 licensees, $0. Then the four questions that actually bring people here.

Not what the page contains, not what it does. What the reader walks away with. The same sentence for every door, with one word changed, is a feature rather than a shortcut - it makes the set legible.

Four figures about the resource, not the statute

The old hero printed 3,000 / 1,750 / 500 / 104. A registered associate knows those. Print what they do not know: how much is here, how well checked it is, what it costs.

05

Start here - the four questions that bring people

Taken from what people actually search and post, phrased as they phrase it. This is the block that turns a hub into a route, and it is where a cold arrival self-selects without being asked to.

06

The shelf, grouped and annotated

Twenty flat cards is a wall; five headed groups is a table of contents. Each card carries what that page says AT THIS STAGE, from the registry, not the page's own summary - which is the whole difference between a stage hub and a re-listed topic hub.

Which county to apply to first, from what each one actually paid

Where the tool goes. If a door has an interactive piece — the associates ledger, the students lookup — it sits after block 05, not before it. The tool is what makes somebody stay; the first five blocks are what stops them leaving.

And the words. Requirement, not gate. Checkpoint, not gate. If a sentence needs the reader to learn a metaphor before it means anything, it is the wrong sentence for a page somebody found while worried.

04

How somebody actually arrives

The doors are five pages. Almost nobody lands on one. This is the journey the architecture has to serve, and the third step is the part that does not exist yet.

Step 01

A search, with a specific worry

“can amft hours be unpaid california”. Not a browse. One question, asked at the worst moment.

Step 02

A leaf page, deep in the site

They land on the wage-claim page. It answers the question well. They have no idea nineteen other pages were written for them.

Step 03

The band tells them where they are

One line above the headline naming the stage, one block below it naming what people read next. This is the piece that does not exist yet, and it is on 200 pages rather than five.

Step 04

The door, and the shelf

Now the hub is a destination rather than a page nobody found. They arrive already knowing why they are there.

Which is the argument for building the band before the remaining four doors: a door nobody can find is worth less than a signpost on two hundred pages.

06

URLs and data

The namespace

/for/associates.html — live. Then /for/deciding.html, /for/students.html, /for/licensed.html, /for/practice-owners.html.

One level, not two. The passes list the site root plus each directory one level down and do not recurse, so /for/associates/index.html would be invisible to every one of them. This was found the hard way.

Two keys per page

"stages": ["associate", "student"],
"stage_note": {
  "associate": "What the claim is worth, and the 30-day clock.",
  "student": "Why a placement cannot ask you to work unpaid."
}

The note is mandatory, and that has a consequence worth stating: it makes bulk tagging impossible. Two hundred honest one-line annotations cannot be written in one pass, and generated ones would be exactly the thin duplicate the field exists to prevent. So a page is tagged when a door that lists it is built. 20 are done.

Three things already shipped that this depends on. "for" is in SUBDIRS in all forty passes, and _dev/subdirs_check.py now fails the build if they ever disagree. registry_sync.py preserves keys it does not own — it was deleting the tagging on the first run, silently. And _dev/plain_gates.py made 309 replacements across 199 pages.

07

Build order

Changed from P2 in one place, and the journey above is the reason: the band comes before the remaining doors.

  1. SUBDIRS, the tagging, and the plain words done

    The configuration line first, so nothing built afterward is invisible. Then the two registry keys, and the sitewide sweep that replaced a metaphor with two plain nouns.

    Shipped 12 August. 202 pages, 0 failures.

  2. /for/associates — the reference implementation done

    Loudest room, twenty pages ready. Built, reviewed, and corrected twice - which is what makes it worth copying rather than the first draft of it.

    Live. The pattern in section 01 is this page.

  3. The band, on 200 pages, and the home page repointed

    Step 3 of the journey. A door nobody can find is worth less than a signpost on two hundred pages, and this is one line in the breadcrumb pass plus one block in the footer pass.

    200 pages gain a stage line and a next-step block.

  4. /for/students — the practicum door

    Runs entirely off data that shipped on 11 August. The most shareable single thing in the set: look up your program, read its own words about who finds your site.

    One door, one new lookup, no new research.

  5. /for/deciding — the largest shelf

    73 pages, lowest urgency. It benefits from the two doors above already existing, because half of what it routes to is them.

    One door. The set reads as a set.

  6. /for/licensed — what changed

    Blocked only on the advertising-rule and telehealth pages, both already on the approved editorial list. Built as a change log, not a masthead: a change log cannot go stale, because staleness is its content.

    One door, plus the two pages it needs.

  7. /for/practice-owners — last

    Eight pages, three of which belong to the licensed door. Opening it now is how a hub becomes a thin duplicate. Four more owner pages and it opens honestly.

    The set closes.

Net new: 4 doors and two page-furniture blocks. Everything else is a view over content that already exists.
