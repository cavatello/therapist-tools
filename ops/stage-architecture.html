01

The verdict, first

Yes — build them. The instinct is correct and the evidence for it is stronger than you probably realize: your own audience has already segmented itself this way, in public, without being asked. But the four-stage list needs three corrections before a line of code is written.

What survives review
  • Stage beats topic as an entry point. Stage predicts which 15 of 182 pages matter to a visitor. Topic doesn't. Someone choosing a grad program and someone deciding on an S-corp have zero page overlap.
  • The shareable-URL argument is the strongest one. Right now there is no link you can paste into a Facebook thread that says "this is for you." Five topic hubs organized by the site's subject matter can't do that job.
  • The stages are real legal statuses, not marketing personas. Trainee, associate and licensee are defined in statute with different rules. That is exactly the condition under which audience navigation is known to work.
What has to change
  • Four stages is really six. And the two you'd add are where the traffic and the money are.
  • "LMFT" is not a stage. It's two audiences with almost nothing in common, and lumping them is the single biggest weakness in the sketch.
  • Don't build the student hub yet. The site currently has close to zero content that only a trainee needs. A hub that links out to other people's pages is the one failure mode that destroys trust.
One-line recommendation

Ship three stage hubs as generated views over the pages you already have — not as standalone sites — and let the other two earn their place by getting content first.

Zero duplicated content. One source of truth. Roughly one builder pass plus a registry field, which is architecture this project already runs five times over.

02

Four pieces of evidence

Not opinion. Two come from your own research, one from the standard reference on this exact IA decision, one from a competitor who has already tested a version of it.

1. The audience already sorted itself

The Facebook content analysis read three California groups totalling 22,800 members. They are not organized by topic. They are organized by stage — a pre-licensed room and a licensed room — and the analysis found the emotional register differs sharply between them:

I am just dealing with a lot of imposter syndrome & questioning if I even learned anything in grad school. Pre-licensed group — a register absent from the licensed group
Group practice owners!! How are you structuring associate caseloads… Licensed group — 2 reactions / 19 comments

Read: the segmentation you are proposing to impose is one your readers have already built for themselves. You'd be matching it, not inventing it.

2. The standard objection, and the escape clause

Nielsen Norman Group's five reasons to avoid audience-based navigation is the canonical warning, and it is worth taking seriously: users don't know which group they're in, labels are ambiguous about for versus about, self-identification is cognitive tax, and overlapping content makes people pogo-stick between sections.

But the same article names the condition under which it does work: when "the content is unique to that audience group" with "enough information specifically for that group to justify its own section."

Read: that sentence is the whole test, and it is testable. §05 below runs it against all 182 pages. Three stages pass. Two don't — yet.

3. The competitor segments — but by the wrong axis

hourjourney.io, the strongest California-specific incumbent, does segment. But it segments by credential — LMFT, LCSW, LPCC, ASW — with a flat "Guides" list underneath. Its nav is: How it works, Pricing, Hours Calculator, Guides, Log in, Start free trial.

Read: credential segmentation is the obvious move and it's taken. Stage segmentation is open, and it's the better axis — because a person's credential is a fact about their paperwork, whereas their stage is a fact about what is going wrong for them this month. Also note what that nav tells you: it exists to sell an hours tracker. Yours exists to be free. The hub can say so.

4. You have already half-built it

The home page carries a Who this is for band with four situations — Considering the path, Registered associates, Solo private practice, Practices with room to grow — each with an "Everything for this situation →" link.

Those links currently go to filtered views of resources.html. They're a promise the site makes and doesn't quite keep: a filter is a list, not a destination.

Read: this is the cheapest possible validation. The taxonomy exists, it's already four-deep, and it's already written in second-person situational language rather than persona jargon. The work is to promote those views into places, not to invent a new scheme.

03

Six objections to the sketch

You asked to be critiqued rather than agreed with. Here is where the concept as stated would go wrong, each with the fix I'd apply.

① "Aspiring → student → associate → LMFT" collapses the two most painful moments

Two transitions carry more anxiety than anything in the four you named, and both get erased by the boundaries you drew:

The gap between graduating and getting a registration number. This is a real status with its own law. Under the Board's 90-day rule, post-degree hours only count if you were Live Scanned at the workplace before gaining them, and the application has to be received within 90 days of the degree award date. Miss the fingerprinting and those hours are simply gone — the Board accepts no alternative documentation. That is a page, arguably a small hub, and it belongs to nobody in your four-stage model.

The first two years after licensure. A brand-new LMFT is deciding whether to go solo at all. A twelve-year practice owner is deciding whether to hire. These are not the same reader, and the site's own content splits cleanly along that line.

Fix: six stages, not four. Details in §04.

② The student hub would fail the only test that matters

Run NN/g's condition against the library. Of 182 pages, the number written specifically for someone currently enrolled and in practicum — trainee hour caps, finding a placement, what to do in the last semester, when to sit law and ethics — is approximately zero. The 66 program reviews serve the applicant, not the enrolled student. They're a different reader who has already made the decision those pages exist to inform.

Fix: hold it. Build five or six trainee-only pages first, then open the hub. A hub that has to send its visitor off-site on the second click is worse than no hub, and this is exactly NN/g's objection ④ — people arrive already suspicious that they're seeing a partial view.

③ "Standalone sites" is the wrong mental model, and this repo has already been bitten by it

You said "almost standalone sites." I'd take the feel and refuse the structure. Genuinely siloed sections mean duplicated chrome, split internal linking, and a second copy of facts that will drift from the first.

That is not hypothetical here. This project has shipped the same class of bug three separate times — data escaped twice, a tuition figure that said "not published" on one card while the same page ranked it at $86,940. Every one of those came from a second copy of something.

Fix: stage hubs are generated views over registry.json, exactly like the topic clusters are today. A page gets a stages: [] field; the hub builder reads it. Nothing is copied, so nothing can drift. You get the destination feel from a distinct accent color, its own masthead, its own share card, and a "you are here" band on every leaf page.

④ Persona labels are the wrong words; search phrases are the right ones

"Aspiring therapist" is a phrase almost nobody applies to themselves. NN/g's objection ② is that labels leave people unsure whether a section is for them or about them — and a noun label ("Students") has that problem badly.

Fix: name every hub in the reader's own situational language, the way the home page band already does. "You're deciding whether to do this at all." "You're counting toward 3,000." The h1 can carry the search phrase; the door can carry the human one.

⑤ Stage is one axis. License type is the other, and it's currently unowned

The site is MFT-first — 66 program reviews, MFT hour rules, MFT statutes. But ASWs and APCCs are a large share of the same rooms, and your own research found the single biggest unserved theme was an APCC denied over four graduate credits from a CACREP school.

Fix: do not build a hub matrix — five stages × three licenses is 15 pages you'll never maintain. Instead every stage hub opens with one honest line naming who it's written for and what differs for ASW/APCC, linking to the specific place that difference is handled. Honest, cheap, and it converts a weakness into a claim nobody else is making.

⑥ A tidy taxonomy is not what makes it a destination

The strongest finding in your own analysis was the method note: comments measure information demand, reactions measure approval. The posts that pulled 87 and 114 comments were not "resources for associates." They were "I have 547 hours and nobody will hire me" and "I'm considering bankruptcy."

Fix: every stage hub leads with the three questions that stage is actually asking — in the asker's words — and only then shows the shelf. A directory ranks content. A destination answers the room. That difference is the entire design brief for §06.

URLs, and how it gets built

The namespace

/for/ — not /stage/, not top-level. It reads correctly when pasted into a sentence, it can't collide with the five existing topic directories, and it signals for you rather than about you, which is NN/g's objection ② answered in the URL itself.

  • /for/deciding/ — open now
  • /for/associates/ — open now · flagship
  • /for/licensed/ — open now
  • /for/students/ — held until it has 5 pages
  • /for/practice-owners/ — held until the queue lands

The four home-page "Everything for this situation →" links repoint from resources.html#where=… to these, which is a two-line change and immediately keeps a promise the site is currently only half-keeping.

The data model

One new field per page in registry.json, alongside the existing topic:

"file": "associate-unpaid-hours-california.html", "topic": "licensure", "stages": ["associate"], "stage_note": { "associate": "What the claim is worth, and the 30-day clock that starts when you quit." }

stage_note is the whole trick. It's the one-line annotation the hub shows — what this page tells you at your stage — and it's why a page can appear under two stages without either hub feeling like a recycled list.

A build_stages.py pass reads the field and writes the five hubs, exactly as build_library.py writes the topic hubs today. Add it to ship.py BUILD; add a guard that every page has at least one stage and every stage has ≥5 pages, so a hub can never quietly open empty.

The "you are here" band — where the destination feel actually comes from

Every leaf page gets a thin band under the breadcrumb: "You're reading this as an associate. ← back to everything for associates." It renders from the referring hub when there is one, and from the page's primary stage when there isn't.

That single band does more for the "standalone site" feeling than a separate codebase would, because it makes the hub feel present on every page rather than only at the entrance — and it costs one pass, no duplication, and nothing that can go stale.
